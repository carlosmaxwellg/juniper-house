# Juniper House Skin & Aesthetics — Brand Voice Guidelines

**Version:** 1.0  
**Updated:** 22 August 2026  
**Status:** Approved-strategy draft for Stage 9 copy  
**Overall confidence:** Medium-high

These guidelines translate the approved Juniper House strategy into repeatable writing rules. The strategic direction is well defined, but the business is fictional and the voice has not yet been validated against customer interviews, real consultation transcripts, or performance data.

## Voice in one sentence

Juniper House sounds like a discerning clinical guide who makes complex choices easier, explains trade-offs plainly, and leaves the client in control.

## We are / we are not

| We are | We are not |
|---|---|
| Calm and assured | Cold, aloof, or ceremonial |
| Specific and transparent | Vague, inflated, or evasive |
| Clinically responsible | Alarmist or falsely reassuring |
| Warmly direct | Overfamiliar, cute, or breathless |
| Refined through restraint | Ornate, status-conscious, or “luxury” for its own sake |
| Consultative | Pushy, urgent, or promotional |
| Respectful of normal appearance | Flaw-finding or insecurity-driven |
| Comfortable saying “not yet” or “not here” | Determined to turn every concern into a treatment |

## Voice attributes

### 1. Clear

**What it means:** Put the decision-relevant fact first. Explain clinical terms in plain English. Name prices, time, recovery, limitations, and next steps without making the reader hunt.

**Evidence:** “Clarity before treatment” is the approved organizing idea. The primary barrier is uncertainty, and conversion depends on clarity about fit, trust, expectations, and control.

**Write like this:** “Your consultation gives you a prioritized plan, expected costs, and the recovery each option may involve.”

**Not like this:** “Embark on a transformative journey designed exclusively for you.”

**Confidence:** High

### 2. Considered

**What it means:** Show that recommendations come from assessment and prioritization, not trends or a predetermined sale. Make room for alternatives, sequencing, and no treatment.

**Evidence:** The approved position is “the clinic for considered decisions,” supported by the Assess → Prioritize → Treat → Review method.

**Write like this:** “The right first step may be a treatment, a simpler home-care change, or a referral.”

**Not like this:** “Choose your perfect treatment today.”

**Confidence:** High

### 3. Reassuring

**What it means:** Reduce fear by making the process legible and preserving agency. Reassurance comes from facts, boundaries, and preparation—not from minimizing risk.

**Evidence:** The customer wants to feel informed, safe, and in control, and fears pressure, unnatural results, and unexpected recovery.

**Write like this:** “You can take your plan away and decide later. Treatment is never required at the consultation.”

**Not like this:** “There’s nothing to worry about.”

**Confidence:** High

### 4. Human

**What it means:** Use direct, respectful language and speak to what the reader is trying to understand. Avoid both medical detachment and faux friendship.

**Evidence:** The brand must feel approachable rather than cold while maintaining clinical credibility.

**Write like this:** “You do not need to arrive knowing the name of a treatment.”

**Not like this:** “Hey gorgeous—let’s fix those problem areas.”

**Confidence:** Medium-high

### 5. Refined

**What it means:** Favor disciplined sentences, useful white space, and a small vocabulary of recurring ideas. Let confidence come from precision.

**Evidence:** The approved personality is discerning, calm, direct, warm, and refined. The strategy defines premium through clarity rather than decoration.

**Write like this:** “A clear plan. A measured pace. Results that still feel like you.”

**Not like this:** “Indulge in our elevated sanctuary of unparalleled rejuvenation.”

**Confidence:** Medium-high

## Voice constants

These should remain true in every context:

- The reader keeps agency. Use “you can,” “we’ll discuss,” and “may be appropriate,” not commands or inevitabilities.
- Explain the reason behind the recommendation or boundary.
- Acknowledge variation in suitability, response, recovery, and outcome.
- Put essential facts before atmospheric language.
- Treat aesthetic care as optional. Never imply that a normal feature requires correction.
- Use concern-led language before treatment-led language.
- Keep the primary action consistent: **Book a consultation**. On the consultation page and inside booking, **Choose a time** is acceptable.
- Say when the clinic may recommend no treatment, a different sequence, or referral.
- Keep fictional status unmistakable wherever profiles, proof, contact details, or booking could be mistaken for a real clinic.

## Tone flexes

The voice stays constant; formality, energy, and technical depth change by context.

| Context | Formality | Energy | Technical depth | Direction |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Homepage and campaign copy | Medium | Medium | Low | Warm, distilled, benefit-led |
| Concern education | Medium | Low | Medium | Neutral, orienting, never diagnostic |
| Treatment detail | Medium-high | Low | Medium-high | Precise, balanced, risk-aware |
| Booking flow | Medium | Medium | Low | Short, direct, action-led |
| Error or interruption | Medium | Low | Low | State what happened and how to continue |
| Risk, contraindication, or urgent boundary | High | Low | Medium | Unambiguous and calm; do not soften the action |
| Simulated confirmation | Medium | Medium | Low | Reassuring but explicit that nothing was booked |
| Portfolio disclosure | Medium-high | Low | Low | Transparent, factual, non-defensive |

## Messaging hierarchy

1. **Core idea:** Clarity before treatment.
2. **Customer benefit:** Understand what fits, what it may involve, and what to prioritize.
3. **Method:** Assess → Prioritize → Treat → Review.
4. **Outcome philosophy:** Healthy-looking, balanced results that still feel like you.
5. **Proof mechanism:** Qualified roles, visible standards, transparent price and recovery, referral boundaries, and a tangible care plan.
6. **Action:** Book the Skin & Aesthetic Planning Consultation.

## Terminology

### Prefer

- client or patient, chosen by context; use **client** for general experience and **patient** where a source, regulation, prescription treatment, or medical risk requires it
- concern, goal, or what you are noticing
- healthy-looking, refreshed, more even, smoother-looking, softened, balanced
- assess, discuss, consider, recommend, prioritize, plan, review
- may help, may suit, can be discussed, designed to support
- recovery or downtime, with a practical explanation
- natural movement and recognizable results
- provider, followed by the specific role
- starting price, likely range, and cost factors
- no-pressure consultation
- model image / illustrative image
- fictional portfolio concept / simulated booking

### Avoid

- flaw, imperfection, problem area, correction of defects
- anti-aging, age reversal, turn back time
- perfect, flawless, poreless, erase, eliminate
- miracle, revolutionary, world-class, best, leading
- painless, risk-free, no downtime, guaranteed, permanent
- toxin-free, chemical-free, clean, detox
- tweakments, glow-up, snatched, frozen, baby Botox
- bespoke when **personalized** or **individual** is clearer
- journey when **plan**, **visit**, or **process** is accurate
- provider superlatives without evidence

### Treatment naming

- First reference: **neuromodulator treatment (prescription botulinum toxin)**. Later: **neuromodulator treatment**.
- First reference: **hyaluronic acid dermal filler**. Later: **HA filler** or **filler**, when unambiguous.
- **IPL photofacial** may be used as the page name; explain that IPL uses broad-spectrum light and is not a laser.
- Use **fractional laser resurfacing**, not generic “laser facial.”
- Use **medical microneedling** only for the clinic’s planned professional procedure; do not imply that all microneedling products are equivalent.

## Style mechanics

- Use sentence case for headings and buttons.
- Prefer short declarative headlines: 3–10 words where possible.
- Lead paragraphs should usually remain under 45 words.
- Paragraphs should usually contain 1–3 sentences.
- Use contractions where natural: “you’ll,” “we’ll,” “isn’t.”
- Address the reader as “you.” Use “we” to describe clinic decisions and responsibilities, not to dominate the message.
- Use the serial comma.
- Use numerals for time, prices, and treatment counts: “45 minutes,” “$150,” “3 sessions.”
- Define technical language at first use.
- Avoid exclamation marks in marketing and clinical copy. One restrained exclamation may be appropriate in a non-medical success state, but it is not the default.
- Avoid rhetorical questions as major headlines. Use questions where they match a real visitor concern, especially in FAQs.
- Never use urgency language based on scarcity unless the scarcity is real and verifiable.

## CTA system

### Primary

- Book a consultation
- Choose a time — only when the consultation value and price are already clear
- Complete simulated booking — final demo action only

### Secondary and contextual

- Explore your concerns
- See how the consultation works
- Compare treatment options
- View pricing and recovery
- Meet the team
- Plan your visit
- Return to Juniper House

### Avoid

- Learn more
- Discover
- Get started
- Submit
- Unlock your transformation
- Book now, when it creates unnecessary urgency

## Copy patterns

### Positioning headline

**Pattern:** Desired confidence + decision benefit  
**Example:** “Know what fits before you decide.”

### Treatment introduction

**Pattern:** What it is + what it may address + why assessment matters  
**Example:** “IPL uses broad-spectrum light to target selected pigment and visible vessels. Skin tone, the type of pigment, and your medical history all affect whether it belongs in your plan.”

### Risk language

**Pattern:** Honest category statement + relevant examples + provider action  
**Example:** “Laser treatment can cause burns, infection, scarring, or changes in skin color. Your provider will assess suitability, discuss the device and settings, and explain the risks that apply to you.”

### No-treatment boundary

**Pattern:** Normalize + explain + redirect  
**Example:** “Sometimes treatment is not the useful first step. We may recommend barrier support, more time, or assessment by a dermatologist or another medical professional.”

### Final CTA section

**Pattern:** Resolve uncertainty + restate deliverable + one action  
**Example:** “Not sure which path fits? Use your 45-minute consultation to compare the options and leave with a prioritized plan.”

### Error message

**Pattern:** What happened + how to fix it  
**Example:** “That time is no longer available. Choose another time to continue.”

## Claims and evidence rules

- Do not publish ratings, review counts, awards, associations, years in practice, treatment totals, outcome percentages, or conversion results without a traceable source.
- Do not present model photography as a patient outcome.
- Every before-and-after image needs treatment, timing, number of sessions, relevant variability, consent status, and an adjacent statement that results vary.
- Use only the indication and risk language supported by the specific product, device, provider scope, and current authoritative information.
- Do not call a product “FDA approved” unless that exact product and use are approved. Device terminology may require “cleared,” not “approved.”
- Never imply that consultation content replaces diagnosis or treatment from a dermatologist or other qualified clinician.
- A real clinic must obtain legal, clinical, privacy, and regulatory review before publishing.

## Open questions and recommended defaults

| Open question | Recommended default for this concept | Confidence |
|---|---|---:|
| Should the brand use “client” or “patient”? | Use “client” for hospitality and planning; use “patient” in medical-risk and regulatory language. | Medium |
| How warm should the homepage feel? | Warm enough to reduce intimidation, with restrained language and human photography; keep copy direct. | Medium-high |
| Should “Clarity before treatment” be the public tagline? | Use it as a recurring supporting line, not necessarily the homepage H1. | High |
| How technical should treatment pages be? | Explain mechanism in one plain-language paragraph, then prioritize fit, recovery, risks, and alternatives. | High |
| Should fictional provider names appear? | Yes, only with an adjacent “fictional profile” disclosure and no unverifiable achievement claims. | Medium |
| Should pricing use “from”? | Yes where treatment scope varies, paired with typical range or cost factors where known. | High |

## Pre-publish checklist

- Does the copy help the reader make a decision rather than merely admire the brand?
- Are price, time, provider, downtime, and important limitations easy to find?
- Is every medical outcome appropriately qualified?
- Are serious risks visible in the main page flow?
- Does the reader remain in control?
- Are generic luxury language, insecurity framing, and false urgency absent?
- Are fictional profiles, model images, demonstration contact details, and simulated booking clearly labeled?
- Has a qualified clinician reviewed any copy intended for a real clinic?
